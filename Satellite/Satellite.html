Space Telescope

A space telescope is a telescope placed in outer space to observe astronomical objects. Space telescopes avoid various disturbances in electromagnetic waves caused by the Earth's atmosphere, including the absorption or scattering of certain wavelengths of light, obstruction by clouds and refraction due to Earth's atmosphere. Unlike ground based observatories, space telescopes are free from light pollution.

The first operational space telescopes were the American Orbiting Astronomical Observatory, OAO-2 launched in 1968, and the soviet Orion 1 ultraviolet telescope aboard space station Salyut 1 in 1971.

List of Space Telescope

This list of space telescopes is grouped by major frequency of electromagnetic wavelength: Gamma ray, X-ray, Ultraviolet, Visible, Infrared, Microwave and Radio. Some telescopes collect particles, like Cosmic ray nuclei or electrons, as well as instruments that aim to detect Gravitational waves. Satellites that were sent beyond Earth with a mission are excluded.

Gamma Ray

Gamma ray is a penetrating form of electromagnetic radiation arising from high energy interactions like the radioactive decay of atomic nuclei or astronomical events like solar flares. It consists of the shortest wavelength electromagnetic waves, with a wavelength of less than 10 picometers (10-11 m) and a frequency of more than 30 exahertz (3×1019 Hz). Gamma rays are produced by some of the most energetic and violent processes in the universe, such as supernova explosions, neutron star collisions, and black hole accretion disks. Gamma-ray telescopes are designed to detect and study these high-energy photons, providing valuable insights into the nature of the universe and the fundamental forces that govern it.

X-Ray

X-rays are a form of high-energy electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths ranging from 0.01 to 10 nanometers (10-11 to 10-8 meters) and frequencies between 30 petahertz (3×1016 Hz) and 30 exahertz (3×1019 Hz). They are produced by high-energy processes such as the acceleration of charged particles, nuclear reactions, and interactions between matter and radiation. X-ray telescopes are designed to detect and study these high-energy photons, providing valuable insights into the structure and dynamics of celestial objects, including black holes, neutron stars, and supernova remnants.

Ultraviolet

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation is a type of electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths shorter than visible light but longer than X-rays, typically ranging from about 10 nanometers (10-8 meters) to 400 nanometers (4×10-7 meters). UV radiation is produced by various astronomical processes, including the emission from hot stars, supernova remnants, and active galactic nuclei. Ultraviolet telescopes are designed to detect and study these high-energy photons, providing valuable insights into the composition, temperature, and dynamics of celestial objects.

Visible

Visible light is the portion of the electromagnetic spectrum that is visible to the human eye, with wavelengths ranging from approximately 400 nanometers (4×10-7 meters) to 700 nanometers (7×10-7 meters). It is produced by various astronomical processes, including the emission from stars, galaxies, and nebulae. Visible light telescopes are designed to detect and study these photons, providing valuable insights into the structure, composition, and dynamics of celestial objects.

Infrared

Infrared (IR) radiation is a type of electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths longer than visible light but shorter than microwaves, typically ranging from about 700 nanometers (7×10-7 meters) to 1 millimeter (10-3 meters). It is produced by various astronomical processes, including the emission from cool stars, interstellar dust, and molecular clouds. Infrared telescopes are designed to detect and study these photons, providing valuable insights into the temperature, composition, and dynamics of celestial objects.

Microwave

Microwave radiation is a type of electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths ranging from about 1 millimeter (10-3 meters) to 1 meter (10-2 meters). It is produced by various astronomical processes, including the emission from cosmic microwave background radiation, interstellar dust, and molecular clouds. Microwave telescopes are designed to detect and study these photons, providing valuable insights into the early universe, cosmic microwave background radiation, and the large-scale structure of the universe.

Name of Telescope Space agency Launch Date Location

Wilkinson Microwave Anisotropy Probe (WMAP) NASA 30 Jun 2001 L2 Lagrange point (1,500,000 km)

Radio

Radio waves are a type of electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths longer than microwaves, typically ranging from about 1 millimeter (10-3 meters) to 100 kilometers (105 meters). They are produced by various astronomical processes, including the emission from pulsars, quasars, and cosmic microwave background radiation. Radio telescopes are designed to detect and study these long-wavelength photons, providing valuable insights into the structure and dynamics of celestial objects, as well as the large-scale structure of the universe.

AstroSat ISRO 28 Sep 2015 Earth orbit (650 km)

Particle

Particle telescopes are specialized instruments designed to detect and study high-energy particles, such as cosmic rays, electrons, and other subatomic particles. These telescopes are typically placed in space to avoid interference from the Earth's atmosphere and magnetic field, allowing for more accurate measurements of cosmic particle fluxes and energies. Particle telescopes provide valuable insights into the nature of cosmic rays, their origins, and their interactions with matter in the universe.

Gravitational Waves

Gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime caused by the acceleration of massive objects, such as merging black holes or neutron stars. These waves carry information about the dynamics of these cosmic events and can be detected by specialized instruments known as gravitational wave detectors. Gravitational wave telescopes are designed to measure these tiny distortions in spacetime, providing valuable insights into the nature of gravity, the structure of the universe, and the fundamental forces that govern it.

Name of Telescope Space agency Launch Date Location
Laser Interferometer Space Antenna (LISA) ESA 2028 L2 Lagrange point (1,500,000 km)
